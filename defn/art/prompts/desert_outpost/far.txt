Create the far distance layer of a modular tiling background set for DEFN, a contemporary-to-near-future
military belt-scroller. This image is not a scene. It is one shallow horizontal band of distant desert
landforms seen across an open basin, drawn against a completely flat, uniform, fully saturated pure green
field (RGB 0, 255, 0) which fills every part of the frame the landforms do not occupy. The green is a cut-out
backdrop that will be removed automatically and replaced by a separately drawn sky behind it; it is not sky,
not land, and not vegetation, and it must never be shaded, graded, textured, lightened, darkened, or blended
into.

Read the layout requirement before anything else, because three earlier attempts failed on it and on nothing
else. The frame is divided horizontally into three equal parts. **The upper two thirds are pure flat green and
hold no artwork whatever.** All of the drawing lives in the bottom third, and the tallest landform in the
image reaches only just into the bottom of the middle third. This is not a composition preference, it is
arithmetic: the band is stretched to its final height in the game, so a band drawn tall in the canvas becomes
a narrow tile that visibly repeats on screen, and a band drawn shallow becomes a wide one that does not. Do
not fill the frame with landscape. Draw a low strip of distant country along the bottom and leave the rest
empty.

The bottom edge of the frame is fully opaque desert floor across its entire width, with no green reaching it
anywhere. The landforms sit on that floor and the floor runs unbroken from the left edge to the right edge.

The backdrop is green rather than magenta on purpose. This biome's palette runs through rose-brown and
mauve-grey sandstone, which sits close to magenta and would key out semi-transparent in the middle of solid
rock. Nothing in this desert is green, so a green backdrop is the one that cannot be confused with the
artwork -- which also means no part of the artwork may be green, olive, khaki, sage, lime, teal, or
yellow-green anywhere.

No reference image is attached, deliberately. Layers in this family are contaminated when they are shown one --
a sky drew the ground plane it was given, a distant band drew the near band it was given -- so the description
below is the whole brief and must be followed on its own.

Understand where this layer sits, because it changes what matters. A run of eroded sandstone shelves and
boulders stands in front of it and hides most of it. What the player actually sees is the top edge of this
band rising above those rocks, and narrow vertical slivers of it through the gaps between them. So the
silhouette along the top edge is the most important thing in the image, and the band must stay interesting at
a *fine horizontal scale*: a viewer reading it through a gap a few metres wide should still see something
worth looking at. Broad slow masses spanning a quarter of the width are wasted here.

Draw it as authored 2D digital cartoon illustration: clean hand-inked contours, broad flat local-colour
regions, and restrained two-value cel shading. Use noticeably finer and lighter linework than a near object,
and very little interior detail -- two or three interior lines to a landform and no more. There are no fine
gullies, no networks of erosion cracks, no scree striation, no individually drawn rock faces, and nothing
described at a scale smaller than a whole terrace. Depth comes from value grouping and desaturation: keep the
whole band low in contrast, cool, and light, close in value to a pale hazy sky, with forms reading as grouped
masses rather than as individually described rocks. Every silhouette edge is still a hard cel edge with a
crisp contour; distance means lower contrast and a lighter, cooler value, and it never means softness, blur,
haze, mist, fog, fading, or transparency. There is no atmospheric veil in front of the landforms, between
them, or along the skyline, and no part of the band dissolves toward the backdrop.

Compose the band as a long, low, distant desert horizon running the full width, built from a varied vocabulary
rather than one repeated shape:

  * flat-topped mesas with level crowns, read as two or three stacked horizontal strata bands and nothing finer
  * eroded escarpment walls that step down in two broad terraces
  * rounded, weathered hill shoulders with soft irregular crests
  * shallow wind-shaped dune backs, drawn as long low curves with a single crisp crest line
  * dry gully mouths cut into the base of a slope as simple dark notches
  * wide skirts of talus fanning out from the foot of the harder rock, as plain triangular masses

Keep it low and horizontal. The band's height varies gently and often across the width, so the skyline is a
continuously interesting line rather than a flat one, and it drops low enough between some of the masses that
wide gaps of clear green open up along it and sky will read through. Vary the *kind* of edge too: a hard level
mesa crown next to a soft rounded shoulder next to a dune curve reads far better at a distance than a run of
similar bumps.

Deliberately place no landmark. There is no tall isolated butte, no spire, no pinnacle, no needle, no rock
chimney however small, no arch, no dominant peak, no lone mast, and no single feature the eye can lock onto,
because this layer repeats and anything distinctive announces itself every time it comes round. Variety here
means many small differences evenly spread, not one memorable thing.

Every part of the band must be unique. Do not repeat a landform arrangement, a crest shape, or a distinctive
mass anywhere across the width, and do not build the run out of a repeating pattern of any kind. The
seamlessness asked for below is a property of the two outer edges continuing into one another, and must never
be achieved by making the interior periodic: a band whose left half recurs in its right half puts the same
shape on screen twice at once, which is far more obvious than any edge seam.

Light it with flat, even, diffuse light that is identical at the left edge, the centre, and the right edge.
There is no directional sun, no raking light, and no lateral brightness gradient whatever. **No landform casts
a shadow onto another landform or onto the ground, in any direction.** Shading is limited to a single gentle
tone change between a form's upper surfaces and its vertical faces, applied identically to every form in the
image. A strip lit from one side cannot be made to tile, because its two ends can never be reconciled, and a
run of cast shadows all falling the same way is exactly that.

The same rule governs colour. The left third, the middle third and the right third of the image must have the
same average hue, temperature and brightness. Every rock colour in the palette appears in all three, in a
different order. Do not let the band drift from cool at one edge to warm at the other.

Use a hazy-distance version of the desert palette: pale dusty blue-grey, washed sand-lilac, soft warm bone,
muted clay-tan, and pale ochre, with soft slate-brown contours. Keep it light and desaturated throughout --
this is the furthest thing in the world. Use no green of any kind, and no pure white.

Build the wrap into the composition rather than hoping for it. The left-most 4% and the right-most 4% of the
frame are the same quiet, plain, evenly toned stretch of open desert floor with no landform standing on it:
same value, same colour, same height of ground, nothing crossing either boundary, and the two margins
interchangeable with one another. Everything worth looking at sits between them. Three earlier attempts drifted
in tone from one side of the frame to the other, which puts a visible vertical line on screen every time the
band comes round.

Make the band perfectly seamless from left to right for continuous horizontal scrolling. The skyline rhythm,
band height, mass distribution and every shape crossing one boundary must continue naturally at the opposite
boundary, and the bottom edge stays at one constant height across both boundaries. Keep roughly the outer 10%
on both sides compositionally plain. Construct a true continuous wraparound rather than placing matching masses
at both edges; the result must stay convincing when repeated four times in a row.

Deliver only the low landform band and the green field: no near rocks, no road, no sky, no clouds, no horizon
line drawn as a rule, no buildings, no ruins, no vehicles, no people, and no gameplay props.

The band carries no writing of any kind. Do not draw numerals, digits, letters, words, logos, signage,
symbols, or any mark that resembles a written character, in any orientation, anywhere in the frame.

Avoid photorealism, realistic 3D rendering, PBR materials, photographic rock or sand texture, perspective
convergence, a vanishing point, lens distortion, depth of field, bloom, lens flare, film grain, painterly
concept-art brushwork, sketchy lines, noisy hatching, dense erosion or stratum detail, cast shadows, a mountain
range, tall peaks, landmarks, fantasy canyon spires, natural arches, alien formations, American-Western
imagery, cacti, palms, green vegetation, semi-transparent or glowing edges, soft airbrushed halos around the
landforms, green tinting or green reflections on the artwork, a graded or clouded backdrop, artwork above the
lower third of the frame, green reaching the bottom edge, visible tiling seams, and mirrored edge masses.

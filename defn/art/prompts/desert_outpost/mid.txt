Create the midground layer of a modular tiling background set for DEFN, a contemporary-to-near-future military
belt-scroller. This image is not a scene. It is one continuous horizontal **bank of desert terrain** -- rock,
boulders, dry earth and the remains of an old outpost -- drawn against a completely flat, uniform, fully
saturated pure green field (RGB 0, 255, 0) which fills every part of the frame the bank does not occupy. The
green is a cut-out backdrop that will be removed automatically and replaced by separately drawn sky, distance
and ground layers; it is not sky, not land, and not vegetation, and it must never be shaded, graded, textured,
lightened, darkened, or blended into.

**Before anything else: all of the drawing lives in the bottom two fifths of the frame.** The upper three
fifths are pure flat green and hold no artwork whatever. This is not a composition preference, it is
arithmetic. The drawn band is stretched to its final height in the game, so the *shallower* it is drawn here,
the wider it is on screen before it repeats; a band drawn tall in this canvas gets squeezed into a narrow tile
that comes round every screen. Draw the run of rock along the bottom and leave the rest of the frame empty.

The shape of this layer is the whole brief, so read this part twice. Within that bottom third, the band
divides into three horizontal zones:

**The middle zone is solid.** Across the entire width, a continuous, unbroken, fully opaque body of terrain
runs from one side of the frame to the other. There are no holes in it, no windows through to the backdrop, no
gaps between one mass and the next, and nowhere in this middle zone may green show through. It is one
connected mass seen edge-on: boulders and shelves resting against and in front of each other, with smaller
rubble and stones packed into every space between them so that the run never breaks. It is made of rock
throughout -- there is no earth, sand or fill anywhere in it.

**The top edge is jagged.** The upper boundary of that body is a strongly varied silhouette against the green:
big shelves and boulders standing up high, deep dips falling away between them, an occasional slab tipped up
above the general line, a brush clump breaking the profile. It rises and falls constantly and by a lot -- the
high points stand two or three times as far above the low points as the low points stand above the bottom of
the frame. Distant hills are drawn on another layer behind this one and read through those dips, so the dips
have to be deep and frequent or that layer is wasted.

**The bottom edge is rock, and this is the part that is usually got wrong.** There is no sand in this image.
No dust, no apron, no pale strip of floor, no bank of earth, no drift and no fill of any kind along the
bottom. The lower boundary of the mass is made of **the bottoms of the rocks themselves** -- the rounded
underside of a boulder, the base of a shelf, the corner of a slab where it rests on the ground -- and below
each of them the frame is clear green immediately.

Draw it as a scalloped, irregular line of rock feet. Where two boulders sit side by side the green comes up a
little way between them; where a big shelf rests flat the line runs level for a stretch and then drops away
again at its end; a small stone tucked in front of a bigger one puts a low bump in it. It rises and falls
constantly, it is made of curves and corners rather than of one continuous sweep, and there is no straight run
anywhere in it. An earlier version filled that zone with a pale sand apron; the game draws its own dirt road
underneath this layer, and the apron simply hid it and read as a shelf.

That is what the whole layer is for: composited over the road, this bottom edge makes the rocks look as though
they are resting on the dirt.

**Every rock keeps its full dark contour all the way around, including underneath.** The outline closes along
the bottom of each boulder and each slab at the same weight it carries everywhere else, exactly as a character
sprite's does, because that is what makes it read as an object standing on the road rather than a hole cut in
a sheet. Do not thin it, fade it or leave it open where the rock meets the ground.

Small bays of green may open between neighbouring feet, and should. What must not happen is a *wide* empty
span under any part of the mass: every rock is either standing on the ground itself or resting against and on
top of rocks that are, so that nothing in the picture could be described as hovering.

Roughly: the rocks fill the drawn band from its jagged top silhouette down to this scalloped bottom edge, and
below the lowest feet the frame is clear green all the way to the bottom edge of the image. All of it stays
inside the bottom third of the frame.

The backdrop is green rather than magenta on purpose. This biome's sandstone runs through rose-brown and
clay-red, which sits close to magenta and would key out semi-transparent in the middle of solid rock. Nothing
in this desert is green, so a green backdrop is the one that cannot be confused with the artwork -- which also
means no part of the artwork may be green, olive, khaki, sage, lime, teal, or yellow-green anywhere, including
the dead brush.

No reference image is attached, deliberately. Layers in this family are contaminated by their references -- a
sky drew the ground plane it was shown, a distant band drew the near band it was shown -- so this one is
described in words and must follow the DEFN house style from that description alone.

**Grade the band by depth: small and near along the bottom, large and far along the top.** This is the single
most important thing about its content. The band is not a flat wall of rock seen side-on -- it is a stretch of
desert receding away from the viewer, so height in the frame *is* distance, and what you draw has to change
size with it. Read the band as three depths that blend into one another with no hard boundary between them:

  * **Along the very bottom, nearest the viewer: small loose stones.** Ankle to knee height on a person, in
    large numbers, with drifts of gravel and chips between them. This is the edge that meets the road the
    characters walk on, and it is what sets the whole layer's scale -- a soldier standing here would be several
    times the height of these stones. Nothing large sits on this near edge.
  * **Through the middle: boulders and slabs.** Knee height to a little above head height, sitting behind and
    among the small stuff, growing steadily bigger as they sit higher in the frame.
  * **Along the top, furthest away: the big outcrops and layered shelves.** These are the largest things in the
    picture -- several times the height of a person -- and they are big *because they are far away and are
    genuinely large landforms*, not because the near rocks were drawn too big. Two or three of them across the
    width, standing behind everything else.

Reinforce the recession the way the house style does everywhere else: as the rock sits higher in the frame,
make the contour lighter and thinner, the interior detail sparser, the contrast lower, and the colour a little
paler and cooler. The near stones along the bottom carry the strongest, darkest outlines and the most
saturated warm colour; the far outcrops along the top are the palest, softest-contrast forms in the layer.
Nothing is blurred and every edge stays a hard cel edge -- distance is a matter of weight and value only.

Get this wrong in the obvious way -- one uniform size everywhere -- and it fails twice over: an earlier version
drew everything at a single size and that size too large, so its smallest rubble came out chest-high on a
soldier and the characters looked like miniatures in front of a cliff. **A wide range of sizes, sorted by
height in the frame, is what this layer needs above all.**

Draw it as authored 2D digital cartoon illustration: clean dark hand-inked contours, crisp graphic
silhouettes, simplified chunky geometry, broad flat local-colour regions, restrained two- or three-value cel
shading, sparse purposeful texture, and gently irregular hand-drawn edges. Weight the linework as a foreground
subject: the outer silhouette of the bank and the major overlaps where one mass crosses another are the
strongest, darkest lines in the image, roughly two to three times the weight of interior marks. Interior lines
are clean and deliberate, never thin, faint, or sketchy. Every line must clarify volume, material, breakage,
or overlap. Those strong contours run around every rock, boulder, slab and remnant, closing each of their
silhouettes completely, along the bottom exactly as along the top and sides.

Build the bank from a varied vocabulary of large desert forms, packed together so they touch and overlap:

  * **Far, along the top:** a big layered sandstone outcrop, its face reading as three or four broad horizontal
    strata of slightly different tone, with a deep undercut shadow where the softer band has weathered back
    beneath a harder cap. Two or three across the width, no more, drawn with the lightest linework in the
    image.
  * **Far:** a blocky slab that has broken off such an outcrop and come to rest at an angle against the mass
    it fell from, with a few large clean fracture faces.
  * **Middle:** clusters of rounded, wind-smoothed boulders of clearly different sizes, knee to head height,
    sitting in front of the outcrops and partly overlapping them.
  * **Near, along the bottom:** loose stones by the dozen, ankle to knee, banked at the foot of everything
    else and spilling forward onto the road. They are the most numerous thing in the image and they carry the
    darkest, heaviest outlines.
  * **Nearest:** drifts of gravel and small chips gathered in the hollows between those stones.
  * A dead desert brush clump -- a bare, brittle, splayed twig mass drawn as a small number of chunky strokes,
    never fine or feathery -- in bleached straw-tan, breaking the top profile here and there.
  * A stub of half-buried concrete wall with a broken top and two or three chunky exposed rebar hooks.
  * A collapsed footing slab tipped out of the ground and lying at an angle.
  * A short slumped line of sagging sandbags gone to rot.

Use each kind more than once across the width but never draw the same one twice: change its size, its
proportions, which way it faces, and what it sits against. Use the concrete remnants sparingly -- two or three
across the whole width, widely separated, each smaller and lower than the rock around it. They must read as
traces left behind, not as a settlement: no intact building, no continuous wall, no compound, no skyline of
structures, no tower, no fence line, no vehicle, and nothing that could be occupied.

Take the variety requirement seriously, because an earlier attempt failed it in a specific and very visible
way. It drew two near-identical undercut shelves, one directly above the other, and immediately to their right
two near-identical boulders, again one directly above the other -- so the run read as a pattern stamped twice
rather than as a place. Three rules follow. **Never place a mass directly above, below or behind another one
that resembles it**; neighbours must be plainly different kinds of thing. **Do not organise the bank into
rows, tiers or a grid** -- no back row and front row, no repeating step. And **the deep undercut or cave-mouth
motif appears at most once in the whole image**; elsewhere the shelves are solid.

Vary the rhythm as much as the shapes. Somewhere the bank is low and quiet for a long stretch; somewhere else
three big masses pile up together; the intervals between the tall points are of obviously different widths,
never an even beat.

Give the bank no pale footing, pad, apron or skirt of sand along its base, and do not lighten the ground colour
underneath it. An earlier version drew one and it read badly: the pale sand carried the dark contour along its
top edge and nothing along its bottom, so the rock appeared to be standing on a flat cut-out disc. The bank
simply meets the floor, the way a character sprite does, and the game draws a soft contact shadow along that
boundary when it composites the layer.

Shade each form with a flat base colour, one clear shadow tone under every overhang, undercut and overlap, and
one restrained lighter tone on its upward-facing surfaces. Apply that convention identically to every form in
the image, as a drawing rule rather than as a light direction: the upper surfaces of a rock at the left edge
are lightened exactly as much as those of a rock at the right edge. Draw no cast shadow on the ground -- the
game adds the contact shadow, and a drawn one would double it.

To repeat the layout requirement, because it is the one most often missed: the rocks occupy only the **bottom
two fifths** of the frame. The upper three fifths are flat green, empty of artwork, edge to edge.

Do not create one dominant landmark, one tallest formation, one arch, one spire, or one focal feature
anywhere; the bank must be evenly interesting across its whole width so that no part of it announces itself
when the layer repeats. Do not repeat an arrangement, a silhouette, a strata pattern, or a distinctive rock
anywhere across the width, and do not build the run out of a repeating pattern of any kind. The seamlessness
asked for below must never be achieved by making the interior periodic: a run whose left half recurs in its
right half puts the same formation on screen twice at once, which is far more obvious than any edge seam.

Make the image perfectly seamless from left to right for continuous horizontal scrolling. The bank runs off
the left edge and continues at the right edge: the height of its top boundary, the height of its bottom
boundary, the colours and the strata all match exactly where the two edges meet, so that the two halves join
into one continuous piece of terrain when the image repeats. Keep roughly the outer tenth on each side low,
quiet and plain -- no tall mass, no distinctive silhouette and no high-contrast feature near either boundary --
so that the join falls through unremarkable ground. The result must stay convincing when repeated four times
in a row.
